Meditation

3 min.

“Our idea of happiness is the main obstacle to happiness.” –Thich Nhat Hanh, Vietnamese monk, peace activist

Meditation is a powerful tool for overcoming grief, crisis, anxiety, and depression because these experiences share a common root: rumination.

The mind tends to replay the same thoughts in a vicious cycle, intensifying negative emotions. By learning to observe your thoughts without engaging with them, you create space for your mind to rest—eventually freeing yourself from what causes suffering.

I’ve followed Buddhist teachings and practiced meditation for years, but over time, my discipline faded. That inconsistency left me emotionally vulnerable and unable to regulate my inner world. The result was deep suffering—not only for me, but also for those closest to me.

Something shifted during this crisis. I stopped blaming others and external circumstances for my pain. Instead, I chose to take full responsibility—to learn from my mistakes and return to meditation with the same commitment I give to my physical health. I’m still learning, but after 2 years of consistent and disciplined practice, I’m starting to see real progress. You can experience this too. When you train yourself to be present, anxiety begins to ease, and day by day, you become more aware of your tendency to overthink—to create suffering by repeating the same internal stories.

when you practice being in the present moment and nothing else, things change, anxiety decreases and each day you become more aware of your tendency to overthink, to find your way into suffering telling yourself the same stories again and again.

Meditation itself is not difficult. What’s difficult is allowing yourself to do absolutely nothing for at least 30 minutes a day in a world that constantly demands productivity. Yet this is exactly what makes it so powerful. Meditation helps us live with intention and strengthens our ability to face life’s challenges—especially those beyond our control.

It allows both body and mind to rest. It reminds us to celebrate being alive and aware. Meditation is a window into the mind—one filled with possibility.

I also like to begin and end each session with gratitude for being alive on this planet, even when life feels painful. I’ve noticed that the more I practice gratitude, the happier I become. Try it.

Meditating in the Midst of Pain

Meditating while facing life’s hardships can feel overwhelming. For some, it may even intensify the pain. Everyone’s experience is different, but I’ve found that surrendering to the experience—rather than resisting it—can be deeply healing.

The sooner you allow yourself to fully embrace your pain and become one with it, the more transformative the process can be. This shift helped me experience fewer relapses and recover more quickly. Giving myself permission to cry as much as I needed was equally liberating.

I’ve spoken with others who shared similar struggles but avoided confronting their pain because it felt too overwhelming. Many of them took longer to heal. That’s why I encourage you to give this approach a chance. When met with awareness, humility, and dignity, pain can become a powerful teacher.

Psychotherapy

4 min.

Psychotherapy is also a powerful tool, but it’s not for everyone. For it to be effective, a certain level of self-awareness is required, along with finding a truly skilled therapist—which is becoming increasingly difficult.

When it comes to choosing the right therapist, there is no one-size-fits-all solution. Every person is different. What works for one individual may not work for another. Our needs, mental frameworks, backgrounds, and cultures all shape what kind of support will be most effective.

I first tried psychotherapy many years ago to address family issues, and it worked. My therapist was one in a million. He was also a Buddhist and incorporated meditation as one of many tools to help his patients. Over time, he became a mentor—and, in many ways, a father figure. He taught me how to face suffering and work through anger using the power of self-awareness. He never took himself too seriously and had a great sense of humor, which I deeply appreciated.

Unfortunately, he passed away in 2017. Every crisis that followed, I had to face using the tools he had given me.

What works for one individual may not work for another. Our needs, mental frameworks, backgrounds, and cultures all shape what kind of support will be most effective.

But this breakup was different. It was long-distance and shaped by many of the worst aspects of modern dating: gaslighting, mixed signals, ghosting, and a lack of real closure. It all unfolded in the digital space—where I also have to show up and perform daily, for many hours—while I was still married. I never imagined I would experience something like this in my forties, after years of a happy marriage, only to find myself filled with resentment toward my husband, blaming him for what happened and unable to take responsibility for my own actions.

It was overwhelming. I felt like I was on the verge of losing my mind—a full-blown mental health crisis.

I desperately needed help, so I began searching for a therapist. I tried three different therapists before finding the one who could truly help me. It wasn’t easy. The stakes felt incredibly high for three reasons:

  1. I had never felt this bad before.
  2. Replacing my former therapist felt almost impossible.
  3. My mental state was fragile, making it difficult to think clearly or objectively.

Therapy is serious work. You are trusting someone with your inner world—your thoughts, your emotions, your vulnerabilities—without filters.

I eventually found my therapist through an Argentinian psychology podcast. Argentina has one of the highest numbers of therapists in the world and takes mental health very seriously. The podcast was so insightful that I decided to try their service—and it turned out to be the right decision. I’d like to take this opportunity to thank Florencia for her support.

Therapy is serious work. You are trusting someone with your inner world—your thoughts, your emotions, your vulnerabilities—without filters. That’s why choosing the right therapist matters. Some therapists can do more harm than good, and many people are not fully aware of this.

If you are considering therapy, understand that it may take meeting with more than one therapist before finding the right fit. That is completely normal. After doing my own research, I created this checklist to help evaluate whether a therapist is right for me. You can use it as-is or adapt it to your own needs and personality.

Therapist Fit Checklist

  • Do I feel safe and respected during sessions?
  • Do I feel heard without being judged?
  • Does the therapist understand the issues I want to work on?
  • Do they explain things in a way that makes sense to me?
  • Do I feel comfortable being honest with them?
  • Are they present, attentive, and engaged?
  • Do they respect my boundaries and pace?
  • Do I leave sessions feeling clearer, supported, or more grounded?
  • Do they help me reflect, rather than just talk in circles?
  • Do they take my goals seriously?
  • Does their approach align with my personality and needs?
  • Am I able to build trust with them over time?
  • Do I ever feel pressured, dismissed, or misunderstood?
  • Are they reliable in terms of scheduling, communication, and professionalism?
  • Can I see myself continuing to work with this therapist?

Red Flags

  • I feel judged, rushed, or invalidated
  • They talk over me or make the session about themselves
  • I consistently leave sessions feeling worse in a confusing way
  • They ignore or dismiss my boundaries
  • I do not feel emotionally safe with them

Community

3 min.

Communities are essential to our well-being and sense of belonging. According to the Harvard Study of Adult Development, happiness is not found in wealth, fame, or intelligence, but in the quality of our social relationships. Strong connections with family, friends, and community improve both physical and mental health, protect us during life’s hardships, and are the strongest predictors of long-term happiness and longevity.

Some people are able to maintain friendships and remain active in their communities while in a relationship. Others, however, make their partner the center of their lives. This can make it much harder to cope with a breakup, sometimes leading to prolonged emotional crises or severe mental health struggles. That is why having a supportive community is critical when navigating difficult moments in life.

In my experience, community was one of the most important factors in my healing. Engaging with different groups—through swim training, the community garden, spending time with friends and family, and meeting new people—made a meaningful difference. Each time I connected with others, the pain didn’t disappear, but it softened and moved into the background.

“Every time we share a piece of our pained inner self with a like-minded friend, we defend ourselves against despair and self-hatred.” –Alain de Botton, A Therapeutic Journey

The most meaningful moments were those when I allowed myself to be fully present—listening to others’ stories, sharing laughter, and giving my full attention to something beyond my own thoughts. Shifting my focus away from rumination and toward genuine connection felt both liberating and deeply healing.

If you are struggling, I encourage you to engage with your friends and family as much as possible. And if you don’t currently feel part of a community, consider seeking one out. Whether through hobbies, interests, or shared passions, there are spaces where you can belong. Building those connections can make life feel richer, more grounded, and more joyful.

Suzanne Simard, the pioneering scientist who uncovered the complex communication systems between trees, suggests that “cooperation, not competition” is what allows species to thrive—and that includes us.

I want to dedicate this project to the people—both near and far—who supported me during my most difficult moments: my husband, my mother and sister, the therapists who listened, and my friends from work, swimming, the community garden, and beyond.

To all of you, thank you for being there when I needed it most. I hope I can do the same for you.

Creativity and Art

3 min.

I don’t believe I can add anything new to the vast body of evidence supporting the healing power of creativity and art. History is full of examples that speak for themselves.

We are all creative and have the capacity to make art. I strongly believe this is part of human nature—even if you don’t consider yourself a creative person.

Art and creativity have been present in my life since childhood. They have always been my safe place, my refuge, and a way to heal during difficult times.

This time was no exception. Creating helped me through my darkest moments—when I felt overwhelmed by painful emotions, flooded with questions, and consumed by the urge to reach out to someone who had hurt me deeply. I wanted explanations, resolution, and a way back to what once was.

In those moments—when I felt empty, when anxiety made it hard to breathe, when guilt felt unbearable—I committed to one simple act to keep myself from falling apart: to create something, anything.

We are all creative and have the capacity to make art. I strongly believe this is part of human nature—even if you don’t consider yourself a creative person.

It wasn’t easy. Gathering the energy to stand up and do something after hours of crying or lying in silence felt almost impossible. Sometimes my mind would shut down completely, leaving my body heavy and unresponsive. Other times, I struggled just to calm my racing heart. But every time I managed to create, it felt like a breath of fresh air—a brief liberation from the thoughts that kept pulling me back into suffering.

Creating opened new paths in my mind. I made it a habit. The outcome didn’t matter. I wasn’t chasing perfection—I was surviving. Showing up was enough.

Each small act of creation, no matter how insignificant it seemed, felt like a victory. That sense of accomplishment grew, and over time, it turned into a habit. I began creating not just to cope, but to feel alive again—to reconnect with the world beyond my pain, a world still full of possibility.

Eventually, this habit led to the creation of this project. By the time I started it, I was already healing and in a much better place. Still, it gave me a way to reflect on everything I had experienced—and a growing desire to share it with others.

Other healing tools

1 min.

Recovering from one of the worst depressions of my life required every tool and form of support I could find. Below are the practices that helped me most. I encourage you to try a few—or create your own—and intentionally schedule them into your routine:

  • Long, mindful walks, preferably in nature
  • Deep, meaningful conversations
  • Visiting museums and immersing yourself in art, science, or both
  • Journaling
  • Blogging
  • Reading
  • Building new, supportive habits
  • Exercising regularly
  • Learning new things
  • Singing out loud
  • Moving your body in ways you enjoy (dancing, swimming, climbing)
  • Spending time with people you love
  • Meeting new people
  • Exploring new places and traveling, if possible

Psychedelics—particularly psilocybin and DMT—have shown promising results in research on depression and anxiety. However, they remain illegal in most countries. If you’re interested, consider exploring eligibility for clinical studies, where these substances are administered in a safe, controlled environment.

The fragility of our mental health

4 min.

Many of us make an effort to care for our physical health—we exercise, try to eat well, and (hopefully) brush our teeth every day. The signs of physical illness or decline are often visible or easy to notice.

Mental health, however, is far more subtle. We don’t always recognize when something is wrong until it begins to surface in our behavior—when we find ourselves arguing over small things, acting in ways that feel out of character, or hurting others and ourselves without fully realizing it. Sometimes, we only become aware of it when life starts to feel unbearable, when the pain becomes too intense to ignore.

It’s surprisingly easy to neglect ourselves—to go long periods without checking in and asking what is causing us stress, anxiety, or sadness. We rarely make time to sit in silence, to be alone with our thoughts, and to truly listen to what our mind and body are trying to tell us.

The signs of physical illness or decline are often visible or easy to notice. Mental health, however, is far more subtle. We don’t always recognize when something is wrong until it begins to surface in our behavior

At the same time, we are constantly navigating pressure from all directions: career expectations, relationships, parenthood, financial stability. On top of that, there is the endless noise of phones and social media—streams of messages telling us how attractive, successful, talented, wealthy, or even spiritually evolved we should be. It can be overwhelming.

I’ve found that one of the most effective ways to navigate this pressure without losing ourselves is to build intentional habits around mental health—just as we do for physical health. What that looks like will be different for each person. It might involve therapy, spending time in nature, climbing mountains, meditating regularly, or anything that helps us connect with our deeper fears, struggles, and anxieties.

At the same time, these practices can help us cultivate appreciation—for the world around us and for our own lives. Even in difficult moments, there is something grounding about remembering the simple fact that we are alive on this planet, surrounded by both challenges and possibilities.

Marriage and the complexities of relationships

5 min.

Human beings are complex, and our relationships reflect that complexity. Whether monogamous, polyamorous, or somewhere in between, any relationship that provides genuine happiness and fulfillment for everyone involved requires effort, patience, compassion, and a deep understanding of both ourselves and our partner(s).

Like most people, I learned this the hard way. I believed I knew myself and understood what I needed to be happy. I thought I listened to my inner voice closely enough to prevent resentment from building and eventually exploding. Yet one day, I woke up feeling empty, lonely, and completely lost.

Raised by a single mother who never believed in marriage, I didn’t believe in it either. Still, I’m pragmatic. I chose to marry for practical reasons—legal benefits and, most importantly, to ensure social security for my partner in a deeply flawed country like ours.

Marriage often carries the expectation of monogamy, and that was something I was never fully comfortable with. I was honest about that from the beginning. The idea of committing sexually to one person for the rest of my life felt daunting. Still, I understood the challenges of maintaining multiple partners within a traditional society while being married. Once again, pragmatism guided my choice: I committed to one person and remained loyal for years—until I didn’t.

True love demands kindness, compassion, empathy, and understanding. But if you fail to offer those qualities to yourself first, you will never be able to give them fully to someone else.

Over time, I realized relationships are like houseplants. They require water and sunlight—some more, some less—but all need consistent care. When we stop giving them what they need, when they need it, they wither.

I don’t consider my marriage a failure. It brought me real happiness for years, and I have never stopped loving my husband—I don’t think I ever will. But like many married couples, we struggled to maintain the right balance between giving and receiving. Balance is everything. Too much or too little of anything can disrupt the body; relationships are no different.

Feeling empty and emotionally drained, longing for the happiness I had lost and desperate to fix my life, I entered a new relationship—one that, given the circumstances, was also destined to fail.

In the beginning of any relationship, both people tend to offer the best versions of themselves. Over time, however, only those who continue to nurture themselves and their partners with consistency and intention—who find the delicate balance between giving and receiving—are able to sustain a flourishing, healthy, and fulfilling connection, whether married or not, monogamous or not.

True love demands kindness, compassion, empathy, and understanding. But if you fail to offer those qualities to yourself first, you will never be able to give them fully to someone else.

Healing from a breakup

3 min.

I’ve come to understand that healing from a breakup is not really about the person who hurt you. It’s about understanding why you ended up with the wrong person—or at the wrong time. It’s about recognizing the ways you were vulnerable without realizing it, the patterns and behaviors you’ve carried for years, and the narratives you keep telling yourself that may prevent you from finding what you truly need to be happy.

As Alain de Botton puts it in his book A Therapeutic Journey: ”…it’s not really about the wrong others have done to us; it’s an honest appraisal of the way we have let them do these things to us, because we have been insufficiently on our own side.

” Healing from a breakup also involves forgiveness—both for ourselves and for the other person. Often, we arrive at these situations not out of malice, but from a lack of emotional awareness and a tendency to seek love before fully learning how to love ourselves.

Science shows that our brains change under the influence of the hormonal surge that comes with “falling in love.” But that stage is only the beginning of truly loving someone. It’s important to distinguish genuine love from dopamine-driven craving and the need for validation.

“…it’s not really about the wrong others have done to us; it’s an honest appraisal of the way we have let them do these things to us, because we have been insufficiently on our own side.” –Alain de Botton, A Therapeutic Journey

When a relationship ends during this heightened state, it can feel like withdrawal. What follows is often a battle against rumination, obsession, and destructive patterns—a battle with our own minds. This is why practices like meditation can be so important in navigating the aftermath.

Modern dating adds another layer of complexity. It requires learning to navigate mixed signals and developing the clarity to recognize when someone genuinely wants to be in your life—and when they don’t. It also asks for honesty with ourselves: Do we truly want this person, and for the right reasons?

Ultimately, healing is about making choices that support our well-being. It’s about setting boundaries, facing uncomfortable truths, and, at times, accepting circumstances we cannot control.

Sometimes, it simply means making peace with reality. If someone no longer loves us—or if we no longer love them—that is part of life. Nothing is permanent. Our task is to let go with as much grace as we can, to carry forward the good memories, and to feel grateful for what the relationship taught us.

A final note: I want to acknowledge my husband. At the beginning of this experience, I suffered in silence, overwhelmed by guilt for the pain I had caused him. Eventually, I couldn’t hide it anymore. His response—kindness, patience, and support—continues to move me. I consider it one of the clearest expressions of love I have ever received.

Modern dating

4 min.

I’m what you might call an “older millennial,” born in 1981. My early dating experiences were a mix of in-person connections and occasional online interactions—but nothing like what we see today. There were no dating apps back then, at least not for young people without money. I met a few strangers on ICQ or through online writing communities, but most of my relationships began the traditional way: meeting people at school, at work, at concerts, or through shared social circles.

Because of that, I arrived late to the world of dating apps—largely unaware of the nuances that define modern dating. At the time, I wasn’t even looking for a relationship, just casual sex.

The abundance of options can make it difficult for people to commit, leading to a sense of disposability—both in how we treat others and how we are treated.

Things I Wish I Knew Before Using a Dating App

  • It can feel like turning yourself into a product—something to present, market, and optimize. I’m still not sure how I feel about that.
  • At the same time, it can feel like shopping—comparing options, evaluating people, and deciding who to “choose.”
  • This dynamic encourages us to highlight our best qualities while hiding everything else.
  • The abundance of options can make it difficult for people to commit, leading to a sense of disposability—both in how we treat others and how we are treated.
  • Even when the intention is “just sex,” deeper emotional connections can form, sometimes unexpectedly.
  • Humans have a remarkable capacity for connection—even at a distance. This should not be underestimated.
  • We are often unaware of our unresolved issues when seeking connection, even in casual contexts.
  • While some people use dating apps with genuine intentions, others do not.
  • When you are emotionally vulnerable or craving connection, it becomes much harder to recognize red flags—even when they are obvious.
  • There are no clear rules. Without caution, or simply due to bad luck, you can find yourself in situations that are emotionally or even physically unsafe.

My takeaway



Looking back, I wish I had been less naive and more cautious. At the same time, there’s a paradox: not knowing can sometimes lead to profound learning. That was my experience.

In the end, I consider myself fortunate. Things could have gone very differently. What made the difference was having the tools and resources to heal and take care of myself after the breakup.

Long distance relationship breakup

5 min.

The first—and only—time I used a dating app was during a trip to Europe. I was feeling very lonely and had an urgent need for connection. After years of traveling alone, I was exhausted, and I felt frustrated with my husband for not joining me again.

I created my account just a few days before flying back home, so I didn’t have the chance to meet any of my matches in person. Months later, I began a long-distance relationship with one of them. What started as something casual quickly became more complex. It felt almost absurd to talk about “just sex” while being so far apart. Instead, we were forced to connect through conversation—messages, chats, and long video calls—and that created a deep and unexpected bond.

We met in person after two months of intense daily communication and weekly video calls. Two months later, we spent a month living and traveling together.

In the end, the relationship fell apart.

I missed all the red flags. What remained was an empty chat window—no more messages, no more video calls, no more contact. The silence of that digital space felt crushing.

As I tried to process the breakup, something felt different—unlike anything I had experienced before. That strangeness is one of the main reasons I decided to write about it. I couldn’t stop wondering how many others had gone through something similar after a long-distance relationship ended.

Looking back, I missed all the red flags. What remained was an empty chat window—no more messages, no more video calls, no more contact. The silence of that digital space felt crushing.

In more “traditional” breakups, there are ways to create distance from the pain. You can avoid certain places, change routines, or even move to a new environment. But what happens when most of your relationship existed in the same digital space you rely on every day—for work, communication, and daily life?

There was no escape. The sense of being trapped in my own pain and loneliness was overwhelming. It felt almost like a form of punishment.

And it wasn’t just digital. Physical spaces became charged with memories too—the streets where I recorded voice notes, the cafés where we had video calls. He seemed present everywhere: in my surroundings and in my mind.

studies have found no significant differences in happiness, commitment, or conflict between long-distance and geographically close relationships.

At first, it felt irrational to be so affected by a relationship that existed largely online. But it wasn’t. Research shows that long-distance relationships can be just as meaningful and intense as in-person ones. For example, studies have found no significant differences in happiness, commitment, or conflict between long-distance and geographically close relationships.

Still, I searched for others who had experienced this kind of digital heartbreak—something that felt uniquely disorienting, especially in a post-pandemic world. I couldn’t find much that reflected what I was going through. That’s why I’m writing this—so that others might feel less alone.

Technology allows us to connect with more people than ever before. But it also has the potential to amplify loneliness, confusion, and emotional distress in ways we are only beginning to understand.

For a while, each day felt like a bad dream. I lost my sense of reality for weeks, and it took months to recover—even with all the healing tools I had available. I often wondered how someone without those resources might cope.

If you are reading this and going through something similar, you are not alone. Many people experience this, even if it’s not widely discussed. I encourage you to explore the Tools for Healing section—what helped me might help you too.
